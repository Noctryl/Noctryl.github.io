---
title: "Why Do Men Hate the Label of Feminism?"
date: 2026-10-30T20:33:00+05:30
tags: ["feminism", "society"]
---

*My parents never taught me about feminism. Maybe they themselves don't know the label.*

As far as I remember, my parents never taught me anything remotely related to feminism. It was never really a subject in our household. As you can probably guess, I was born and brought up in a mildly conservative, or perhaps slightly liberal, family. We never really talked about girls or women, partly because neither my brother nor I had any sisters, and it simply never occurred to us to discuss such things. My friend group was almost exclusively made up of boys, although I did have one female friend during preschool. Our mothers were close friends, and we used to play all sorts of games together.

As I grew older, my friend circle became strictly limited to boys. Although I studied in a co-educational school, we hardly ever talked to girls or even paid much attention to them. We were simply too occupied with ourselves. So, apart from my mother, there were practically no other female figures in my life.

Even though we never discussed feminism or anything remotely related to it at home, I wouldn't say that my parents raised my brother and me with a particularly patriarchal mindset. We were expected to do things ourselves, and there was never any distinction between what boys or girls were supposed to do. I'd say we were raised pretty much like a blank slate, without any strong preconceived notions about either gender, which later turned out to be a good thing for me.

Since childhood, I've had a particularly strong disdain for people who make sexist or misogynistic remarks. To put it under a broader umbrella, I've never really liked people who unnecessarily talk down to women in any way. What's interesting is that I felt this way even though I wasn't particularly raised in a household where we discussed such things. It was something I had to figure out for myself.

I started noticing these things through rather subtle incidents. One day, one of my friends' mothers told her not to go out after 8 p.m. because it wouldn't be safe. *Unsafe? From whom?*

I was allowed to go outside, so I asked my friend why her mother wouldn't let her do the same. She told me it wasn't safe for girls. *Safety from whom?*

That question stayed with me for quite some time. I was around eleven years old at the time, and obviously didn't have a particularly developed understanding of these things, but it kept me thinking. Why were we treating girls and boys differently? Why was society placing restrictions on women instead of holding the people responsible for making them unsafe accountable? This is quite a rabbit hole, and it could go on endlessly if I were to start exploring every aspect of it. Her mother never really gave me a satisfactory explanation, either. Besides, questioning our elders isn't exactly encouraged in our society; in fact, it's often frowned upon. So, once again, I was left to figure things out for myself.

I then started reading more about it. About how women are *marginalised* in almost every aspect of society, how patriarchy is, *unfortunately*, *deeply institutionalised* in our social structures, and how unwilling our governments often seem to be to make things better. I've been furious with the government on several occasions, but when I saw the same misogynistic people occupying prominent bureaucratic and governmental positions, I gradually started losing faith in the system. And that faith hasn't quite returned yet.

Eventually, I found myself identifying with feminism. It wasn't something I had inherited from my family or something I had been formally introduced to at school. It was a perspective I gradually developed through observation, conversations and independent reading. But that also made me curious about something else. If the fundamental principles of feminism are equality and dignity, why does the label itself generate so much resistance, particularly among men? Why are there men who might agree with its underlying principles but still hesitate to associate themselves with it?

Environment plays a big role because humans can be easily influenced, or even manipulated. Human behaviour is shaped by our surroundings, the situations we find ourselves in, the rewards we receive and the social settings we grow up in. And when your environment applauds you for making sexist or misogynistic remarks, it certainly doesn't help. Luckily, my close friends throughout the years have generally been a bunch of genuinely smart and respectful people, regardless of whom they were interacting with. I think that's particularly important because, well, many classrooms are heavily misogynistic, and people actually seem to think it's cool to make comments like these.

Long story short, it's a huge issue here. I've also seen people who pretend to be feminists just to gain approval and portray themselves as the nice, good guy, while holding deeply misogynistic beliefs underneath. This could perhaps become an entirely separate topic about performative or pretentious feminism, but I think the two are closely connected. After all, people often don't want to associate themselves with the label of feminism. Of course, there's a very large section of society that outright dismisses feminist ideas and continues to uphold patriarchal beliefs, but even among those who seem to support the underlying principles, there's often considerable scepticism towards the label of feminism itself.

Maybe this is a collective failure of our society. Feminism was never really introduced to many of us as something that promotes equality, but rather as something evil, supposedly done by women to disrupt the established social order. There's also this conditioning where people actually think that it's a term coined by women, for women, and that if men were to accept it, they'd somehow become more feminine – and, of course, less masculine. Society has never really applauded men who openly embrace feminism. It has always been something to look down upon. And then there's the social media sphere, where everyone seems to be trying to sell their own fake, cheap ideologies just to fit in.

In my humble opinion, if society doesn't make it publicly acceptable for men to embrace feminism and openly condemn patriarchal ideas, the movement isn't going to get very far. Boys are often told to be strong and not to cry from childhood, while feminism is considered something feminine. So, if you're a feminist – boom! You don't have a spine, you're girly, and whatnot.

And humans are, after all, social animals. We seek validation, acceptance and social status from the people around us. If our society doesn't make these things acceptable and instead shames men for openly associating themselves with feminism, I don't see how much is going to change.

Though I also think it would be unfair to place all the blame on individual behaviour. People often make misogynistic remarks under the guise of worrying about what society will think of them. It becomes a negative feedback loop. And the linguistic framing of feminism itself seems to bother some people, too. But that shouldn't be a reason to reject something whose underlying principles are beneficial to everyone, though...

Just a thought.
