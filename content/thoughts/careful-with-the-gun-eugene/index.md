### tragedy, paradoxes, wars.

*from the diary of Eric Waters.*

New Year Eve, 1929

On that day the young Eric left his mother’s house at Copley, near Barnard Castle, where she was the housekeeper for the local country doctor, and went out on foot with the Zetland foxhounds. *“It is a beautiful, eloquent account of the crispness of the air, the snow on the ground and the cry of the hounds, and how a fox was finally killed in a railway cutting,”* he says.

Later on he mentions, *“Caught the United bus to Barnard Castle. Played snooker with Jack. Won Tuppence.”*

This was the diary of eric waters, born in 1913.  
His father, George Henry Waters, was a coal miner, Eric belonged to a working-class family. He was 16 years old when he wrote this memoir.

His life was full of paradoxes.

Eric was a deeply religious Christian and vocal objector when Britain entered WWII in 1939. His objection was rooted in the commandment *Thou shalt not kill.* He firmly believed in pacifism. Because of his stance, he chose non-combat war work and drove an ambulance in London during the Blitz.

*Yet,* he concluded that not fighting Hitler was morally indefensible. He returned to the authorities, withdrew his objection, underwent military training, and became a commissioned officer.

Eric’s ultimate objective was arguably not fundamentally different from that of his earlier pacifist self: he wanted a world in which people weren’t subjected to fascism, violence, and oppression, yet he started to believed violence had become inevitable to defend that world.

It raises a major ethical question: Can violence be morally justified when its purpose is to prevent something worse?

On 18 February 1944, Z Company came under renewed German attack. The battalion’s operational records describe increasingly desperate fighting.

At 11:30 a.m., the war diary recorded the death of Lieutenant Waters and the critical situation of his company.

He died when his younger son was only 5 months old, named Roger Waters, who’d later on become the renowned lyricist and member of the band Pink Floyd.

Roger never saw his father. He grew up in the absence of his father and was solely raised by his mother, Mary Duncan Waters.  
He didn’t grow up remembering his father’s voice, expressions, habits, or conversations. Much of what he knew came from his mother’s story.

In a 2018 interview, Roger Waters recalled a story his mother had told him about the years after the war.

When soldiers returned after the WW-II, they’d arrive on bicycle and collect their children from school. He’d ask his mother

*Where is my father? Where is my man on the cycle?*

Initially, Mary told him he was stationed in Italy, only later on she told him that he died on that war.

At first, Roger thought perhaps being good enough, doing enough, becoming enough could somehow bring back the person who had disappeared.

When Mary told him about his father’s death—he refused to believe it. He imagined going to Italy himself, first with a tractor, then with a double-decker bus, vehicles powerful enough in his childhood imagination, to bring his father back.

He didn’t understand the finality of death yet, believing there might still be something he could do to bring him home. He tried overturning what adults described him as irreversible.

For years, he waited for his father to come back from the war that ended years ago.

In later interviews, Roger spoke about the lifelong need to impress other men, and the constant desire for a father to tell him that he had done well, despite having no memory of what it actually feels to get the approval of father.

He never remembered disappointing his father, making his father proud, telling him that he loves him, yet he nearly spend his entire lifetime wanting to hear those words.

He mourned an entire relationship that never got chance to exist.

In 2000 Guitar World interview, he described a recurring dream in which he had killed someone and was waiting to be punished. Later on, during therapy he came to understand that on some subconscious level, he had felt that he had killed his father.

And his reasoning was devastatingly simple—

*I was born, and he died.*

Of many countless single mothers, her mother Mary played the role of both parents. She was a staunch anti-war activist, passing down her ideology to Roger. She taught him to feel empathy for other human beings.

Later on in his life, Waters joined college, left it and pursued music eventually becoming one of the founding members of Pink Floyd.

In 1971, Pink Floyd recorded their music in the empty amphitheatre of Pompeii, in Italy naming it *Live at Pompeii*, which is the pinnacle of rock performances in my opinion.

The band Pink Floyd performed the song, *A Saucerful of Secrets*. It was divided into four movements—Something Else, Syncopated Pandemonium, Storm Signal and the Celestial Voices.

The song begins with a heavy bassline, depicting its dark, atmospherically buildup. The opening feels strangely unsettled. Cymbals ring faintly in the background, the organ sounds distant, and little fragments of music emerge without ever quite forming into anything. You keep waiting for the music to settle, but it never really does. Something feels wrong, although you can’t quite tell what.

I interpret it as the world slowly losing its ability to keep the peace. Leaders mobilizing people with their cheap and greedy propagandas, leaders and their growing distrust and their sadistic appetite for mongering a war and whatever little diplomacy remains begins to fall apart. One could easily sense the growing feeling that nobody is going to stop what is coming.

The war hasn’t begun physically, but its possibility has already begun to dominate everything, people preparing to live in bunkers, leaders hiding in their safe havens, military drills and what not.

With a quick comes the second movement—Syncopated Pandemonium—mobilization and the battlefield.

Then comes the eruption.

The percussion becomes aggressive, the guitar produces screechy and distorted sounds, and the music develops into a dense, chaotic collision.

Nick Mason’s drumming becomes the physical force driving the whole song now—repeated drum notes—as if signifying the march and mobilization of military. The repeated gong strikes sound like an announcement that the war has begun. Armies are moving, artillery is firing, and the battlefield is no longer something distant or imagined. People were filled with rages, blood on their head to kill the other side soldiers without even knowing or having personal grudge with them.

But the music quickly exceeds the repetitiveness of a march.

It becomes pandemonium.

The distorted guitar and crashing piano sounds don’t They sound like everything falling apart. The ideological conflicts turned into something devastating, the physical violence and murdering each other.

Whatever ideas brought these people to war, they weren’t on their minds now. The fighting has taken over, and nobody seems capable of stopping it.

Then comes the third movement—Storm Signal—the battlefield after the battle.

This is the most disturbing transition of the song.

After all that noise, the music suddenly seems to withdraw into itself. The percussion fades, leaving behind the chimes and Richard Wright’s organ. It’s almost difficult to believe that this is the same piece of music we were listening to moments ago.

The war is over, firing is stopped, but the smoke hasn’t disappeared. There’s no one from the both sides to announce the victory or loss. The battlefield is filled with dread silences. The destruction hasn’t been undone. It showed us a catastrophic reality of war, the soldiers who marched into battle are not returning. The homes they left behind, they would never see them again. Somewhere, a mother is still waiting for a letter; somewhere, a child is still expecting footsteps at the door of his papa.

The silence offers no answers. We don’t know whether the war was worth fighting… whether the sacrifices accomplished anything, or whether the people who died ever understood what they were dying for.

From this tragic scenario, then comes the celestial voices—**the mourning of the dead**

The final section is almost holy its character. Richard Wright’s organ establishes a slow, church chord progression.

The music becomes expansive and deeply reflective.

David Gilmour (lead guitarist and vocalist) then burst into wordless vocals, almost sounding as angelic cries, transcending all languages and barriers.

There is no victory anthem. No celebration of the defeated enemy. No declaration that the sacrifice was worthwhile, but MOURNING.

The voices didn’t belong to any nationality. They could be the cries of British soldiers, German soldiers, or anyone else who never made it home and the distinction makes it overwhelming to bear the pitiful reality it caused.

The devastating reality of war, loss of human life, greedy leaders fighting for their pitiful ideologies pushing people in front lines while staying safe themselves, the tragic loss of people being said as mere sacrifices, children who never saw their father, mothers waiting for their child to come home again, Wives, waiting for their husbands to come, changed something deeply in me. It’s hard to put it into words, but i felt it. People still don’t understand the horrors and the aftermath of wars. A whole generation raised by single mothers. Yet we have leaders who *yearn* for war. We’ve billionaires who would lobby wars to these leaders. *But why?* Because they themselves are never pushed to the front lines. It’s easy to sit in a ballroom while outsourcing war and it’s strategies to bureaucrats, but it’s incredibly and painfully difficult to leave your whole family and life behind just to fight for morale you might not even stand for.

Putin waging war to Ukraine, committing mass genocide and atrocities to Ukrainian people. People spending whole days in subways just to not get bombed. What was their fault?

School girls getting killed in Iran, which pitifully described by military agencies of US and Israel as “false intels”

Trauma travels through generations and is passed down in genes. Just because rich and politicians don’t face it, they never build the empathy for the people involved.

The same Roger Waters continue to spend his whole life yearning for his father, whom he never seen, met or remembered. His father being a devout Christian and staunch pacifist gets killed for the war he once openly opposed.
